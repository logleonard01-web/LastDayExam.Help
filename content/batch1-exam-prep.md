title: How to Plan the Final Day Before an Exam
subject: exam-prep
kind: Cornerstone guide
description: A realistic plan for the last day before an exam: what to review, in what order, and how to protect rest.
vocab: analyze, evaluate, explain
related: last-day-revision-schedule, final-revision-checklist, protect-sleep-before-exams

The day before an exam is not the time to learn an entire course. It is the time to **review what you already know, find the few gaps that matter most, and arrive at the exam rested and organised**. Students who treat the last day as a sorting exercise usually do better than those who try to read everything once more.

## Start by listing what the exam covers

Write down the exam's topics. Use your course outline, the teacher's revision list, or the headings in your notes. If your exam board or teacher published a specification, use that rather than guessing.

Next to each topic, give yourself a quick rating:

- **Secure:** you could explain it to a classmate without looking.
- **Shaky:** you recognise it but make mistakes.
- **Missing:** you cannot do it yet.

This takes about fifteen minutes and tells you where the next hours should go.

## Decide what to review first

Spend most of your time on topics that are both **likely to be tested** and **shaky**. A secure topic needs only a short refresher. A missing topic that is a small part of the exam may be worth a brief look, not a long struggle. A missing topic that carries many marks is worth your best effort early in the day, while you are fresh.

## Use active methods, not rereading

Rereading feels productive because the page looks familiar. Familiarity is not recall. Better options for the last day:

1. **Close the notes and write what you remember** about a topic, then check what you missed.
2. **Do a few practice questions** on shaky topics, and read the explanation for every one you get wrong.
3. **Review your mistakes** from earlier tests, homework or practice papers.
4. **Make a one-page summary** of formulas, definitions and key dates you are most likely to need.

See [using practice questions effectively](/blog/using-practice-questions-effectively/) for how to get the most from the second method.

## Build in breaks and meals

Work in blocks of 25 to 45 minutes with short breaks between them. Eat regular meals, drink water, and spend some time outside if you can. Studying with no breaks does not add hours of useful work; attention fades.

## Prepare everything for tomorrow

Before the evening, set out what you need: identification if required, pens, pencils, a calculator that is allowed in your exam, a watch if clocks are not guaranteed, water and the exam's start time and location. Check the route and travel time. Our [final revision checklist](/blog/final-revision-checklist/) has a full list.

## Stop at a sensible hour

Finish studying early enough to relax, and then sleep. Sleep helps memory consolidation and attention during the exam, so a late night usually costs more than it gives. Read more in [why protecting sleep beats cramming](/blog/protect-sleep-before-exams/).

## A sample shape for the day

This is a template, not a rule. Adjust it to your exam time and subjects.

- **Morning:** list topics, rate them, and work on the highest-priority shaky topic.
- **Midday:** a meal, a walk, then a second priority topic.
- **Afternoon:** practice questions and mistake review.
- **Early evening:** a one-page summary and a light read-through.
- **Evening:** pack, relax, and go to bed at your normal time.

## Common mistakes

- Trying to cover every topic equally.
- Starting a brand-new topic late in the evening.
- Skipping meals or sleep to fit in more hours.
- Comparing yourself with friends who say they have studied more.

## Quick recap

List the topics, rate them, spend your time on the important shaky ones, practise actively, take breaks, prepare your materials, and sleep. A calm, organised last day will not replace weeks of learning, but it helps you use what you know.

=== last-day-revision-schedule
title: Building a Realistic Last-Day Revision Schedule
subject: exam-prep
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How to turn the hours you have left into a schedule you can actually follow.
vocab: outline, justify
related: final-day-exam-plan, concise-revision-notes
practice: mathematics
---
A good schedule is short, specific and flexible. A schedule that packs every hour is almost guaranteed to fall apart by lunchtime, and then you feel worse than if you had not made one.

## Step 1: count your real hours

Subtract meals, travel, breaks and sleep from the hours between now and the exam. What remains is your study time. If you have eight usable hours, plan for about six, which leaves room for delays.

## Step 2: choose blocks, not minutes

Divide the time into blocks of 30 to 45 minutes, each with a clear goal. A goal such as "do ten practice questions on quadratic equations and check each one" is better than "study maths".

## Step 3: assign priorities

For each subject or topic, think about two questions:

- How many marks could it be worth?
- How confident am I?

High marks and low confidence go first. If several subjects are on consecutive days, give most of today's time to tomorrow's exam rather than splitting evenly.

## Step 4: mix methods inside the day

Alternate between reading, recalling from memory, and answering questions. Switching methods keeps attention up and tests your understanding in different ways.

## Step 5: leave a buffer

End with a 30-minute buffer block. If you fall behind, use it. If you do not, relax.

## An example for one subject and three hours

| Block | Goal |
|---|---|
| 1 (40 min) | Recall main topics from memory, mark gaps |
| 2 (40 min) | Fix the biggest gap using notes |
| 3 (40 min) | Practice questions on that topic |
| 4 (40 min) | Review mistakes, update summary sheet |

Take a short break between blocks.

## Common mistakes

- Planning back-to-back blocks with no breaks.
- Leaving "reading" as the only method.
- Treating the schedule as a test of discipline. If something takes longer, adjust and move on.

## Quick recap

Count real hours, set specific goals for each block, prioritise by marks and confidence, vary methods, and keep a buffer.

=== exam-command-words
title: Exam Command Words: What Each Instruction Asks For
subject: exam-prep
kind: Vocabulary reference

vocab: analyze, evaluate, compare, contrast, explain, describe, define, justify, summarize, calculate
related: essay-exam-strategy, checking-answers-before-submission
practice: english
---
Command words are the verbs that tell you what to do in an exam question: *describe*, *explain*, *evaluate* and so on. Many lost marks come from answering a different question than the one asked. Writing an explanation when the question says "state" wastes time. Writing a description when it says "evaluate" misses the marks.

**Important:** command-word meanings can differ between subjects, exam boards and mark schemes. Treat this guide as a general orientation, and check the glossary your own exam board publishes.

## Short-answer words

- **[State](/vocabulary/state/), [identify](/vocabulary/identify/), [define](/vocabulary/define/):** give a brief fact, name or meaning. No reasoning needed.
- **[Calculate](/vocabulary/calculate/), [determine](/vocabulary/determine/):** produce a numerical answer, usually with working shown.
- **[Estimate](/vocabulary/estimate/):** give an approximate value using reasonable assumptions.

## Words that ask for an account

- **[Describe](/vocabulary/describe/):** say what something is like or what happens. Mention features, steps or patterns.
- **[Outline](/vocabulary/outline/):** give the main points briefly.
- **[Summarize](/vocabulary/summarize/):** condense the key ideas.

## Words that ask for reasons

- **[Explain](/vocabulary/explain/):** give reasons or causes. Good explanations often use *because*, *so* and *therefore*.
- **[Justify](/vocabulary/justify/):** support a decision or answer with evidence or reasoning.
- **[Elaborate](/vocabulary/elaborate/):** add detail or examples to a point.

## Words that ask for comparison

- **[Compare](/vocabulary/compare/):** similarities and differences.
- **[Contrast](/vocabulary/contrast/):** mainly differences.
- **[Distinguish](/vocabulary/distinguish/):** show what makes two things different.

## Words that ask for judgment

- **[Analyze](/vocabulary/analyze/):** break into parts and show how they connect.
- **[Evaluate](/vocabulary/evaluate/) and [assess](/vocabulary/assess/):** weigh strengths and weaknesses, then reach a conclusion.
- **[Discuss](/vocabulary/discuss/):** consider more than one point of view.
- **[Critique](/vocabulary/critique/):** give a reasoned judgment of strengths and weaknesses.

## A simple method for any question

1. Circle the command word.
2. Underline the topic.
3. Check the marks. More marks usually mean more points or more development.
4. Write a one-line plan before you answer.

## An example

Take the topic *photosynthesis*:

- *State the gas released by photosynthesis.* One word: oxygen.
- *Describe the process of photosynthesis.* Inputs, outputs, where it happens.
- *Explain why photosynthesis slows in low light.* Link light energy to the rate of the process.
- *Evaluate an experiment on photosynthesis.* Strengths, weaknesses, improvements.

## Quick recap

Read the command word first. Match the depth of your answer to the verb and the marks. When unsure, check your exam board's published definitions.

=== final-revision-checklist
title: A Final Revision Checklist
subject: exam-prep
kind: Revision checklist
description: A practical checklist for the night before and morning of an exam.
vocab: identify
related: final-day-exam-plan, protect-sleep-before-exams

---
Use this list as a prompt, not a test. Skip anything that does not apply to your exam.

## Know the exam

- I know the date, start time and location.
- I know how long the exam lasts and how marks are shared across sections.
- I know the question types: multiple choice, short answer, essay, calculations.
- I know whether notes, formula sheets or calculators are allowed.

## Know the content

- I have a list of the topics that might be covered.
- I have reviewed the topics I rated shaky.
- I have a one-page summary of formulas, definitions and key facts.
- I have corrected mistakes from recent practice.

## Prepare materials

- Identification, if required.
- Several pens and pencils, an eraser, a ruler if needed.
- An approved calculator with working batteries.
- A watch that is allowed in the exam room.
- A clear water bottle if permitted.

## Prepare yourself

- I have eaten a proper meal.
- I have planned my route and travel time with a buffer.
- I have set an alarm and a backup.
- I am going to bed at a reasonable hour.

## On the morning

- Eat breakfast and drink water.
- Read your one-page summary once, calmly. Do not start new topics.
- Arrive early enough to settle.

## In the exam

- Read the instructions and the marks for each question.
- Circle command words.
- Start with questions you can answer, then return to harder ones.
- Leave time at the end to check your work.

See [allocating time during a test](/blog/allocating-time-during-a-test/) and [checking answers before submission](/blog/checking-answers-before-submission/).

=== multiple-choice-strategy
title: Strategies for Multiple-Choice Exams
subject: exam-prep
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How to read multiple-choice questions carefully, eliminate options, and manage time.
vocab: identify, infer
related: allocating-time-during-a-test, checking-answers-before-submission
practice: science
---
Multiple-choice questions test recognition and reasoning at the same time. A few habits reduce careless errors without any need for tricks.

## Read the question before the options

Cover the answers and try to form your own answer first. When you then look at the options, you are less likely to be pulled toward a plausible but wrong choice.

## Watch for qualifying words

Words such as *not*, *except*, *always*, *never*, *only* and *most likely* change the meaning of the question. Underline them. A statement can be true in general yet wrong because it says "always".

## Eliminate, then decide

If you cannot see the answer, remove options you know are wrong. Even removing one improves your odds if you must guess. Check whether your exam penalises wrong answers. Many do not, but you should read the instructions to be sure.

## Check each option against the question

An option can be true but not answer the question asked. For example, a question asking for the *cause* of an event may list a true *effect* as a distractor.

## Calculations

Do the calculation before looking at the options, then find your result. If your result is not listed, recheck units and arithmetic. Common distractors come from typical mistakes: forgetting to convert units, mixing up addition and multiplication, or rounding too early.

## Manage time

Give each question an approximate time. If one is taking too long, mark it, choose a provisional answer and move on. Return at the end if time remains.

## Changing answers

Change an answer if you find a specific reason it is wrong, such as a misread word or an arithmetic slip. Avoid changing answers only because of a vague worry.

## Quick recap

Form your own answer first, underline qualifiers, eliminate wrong options, check that your choice answers the exact question, and pace yourself.

=== essay-exam-strategy
title: Preparing for Essay Examinations
subject: exam-prep
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How to plan, structure and time an essay answer under exam conditions.
vocab: analyze, discuss, evaluate, justify
related: essay-structure-basics, thesis-statements, exam-command-words
practice: english
---
An essay exam rewards a clear argument supported by evidence. You do not need to write everything you know. You need to answer the question that was asked.

## Before the exam

- **List likely themes** from your course and write a short outline for each.
- **Collect key evidence**: dates, quotations, formulas, examples, case studies, depending on the subject.
- **Practise planning.** Take a past or sample question and write a five-minute plan without writing the essay.

## In the exam: the first five minutes

1. Read the question twice.
2. Circle the command word and underline the topic. See [exam command words](/blog/exam-command-words/).
3. Write a short plan: a one-sentence answer to the question, then three or four points with the evidence for each.

## Structure

- **Introduction:** respond to the question directly and state your main position in one or two sentences.
- **Body paragraphs:** one point per paragraph. State the point, give evidence, explain how the evidence supports the point, and link back to the question.
- **Conclusion:** answer the question again, summarising the reasoning. Do not add new points.

See [essay structure basics](/blog/essay-structure-basics/) and [thesis statements](/blog/thesis-statements/).

## Time

Divide time by marks. If an essay is worth a third of the exam, give it roughly a third of the time, including planning and a short check. Stop writing a paragraph when your time for it is up, even if you have more to say.

## Common mistakes

- Writing everything you know about the topic instead of answering the question.
- Beginning without a plan and drifting.
- Giving evidence without explaining why it matters.
- Running out of time before a conclusion. A short conclusion is better than none.

## Quick recap

Plan first, answer the question directly, one point per paragraph with evidence and explanation, watch the clock, and finish with a conclusion.

=== allocating-time-during-a-test
title: Allocating Time During a Test
subject: exam-prep
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How to divide the exam time across questions using marks as a guide.
vocab: calculate, estimate
related: multiple-choice-strategy, checking-answers-before-submission
practice: mathematics
---
Running out of time is a common reason for lost marks. A simple method keeps you on track.

## Use marks to set time

If an exam has 100 marks and lasts 120 minutes, each mark gets about 1.2 minutes. A 10-mark question gets about 12 minutes; a 4-mark question about 5 minutes. Keep 10 to 15 minutes at the end for checking, so adjust the working time to match.

**Worked example.** An exam lasts 90 minutes and has 60 marks. You want 10 minutes for checking.

- Working time: 90 − 10 = 80 minutes.
- Time per mark: 80 ÷ 60 ≈ 1.33 minutes.
- A 6-mark question: 6 × 1.33 = 8 minutes.

## Note your checkpoints

Write a few checkpoints on your paper when the exam starts. For instance, "Question 10 by 45 minutes". Glance at them occasionally.

## If you get stuck

Give a question its allotted time. If you have not found a path, write what you can, mark it, and move on. A partial answer can earn marks, and you may think of the solution later.

## Order of work

Many students start with the questions they are most confident about to build momentum. This is a good approach if the exam does not require a particular order. Read the instructions first.

## Quick recap

Calculate time per mark, reserve checking time, set checkpoints, and move on when stuck.

=== checking-answers-before-submission
title: Checking Your Answers Before You Submit
subject: exam-prep
kind: Supporting explainer
description: A short routine for catching avoidable errors in the last minutes of an exam.
vocab: calculate, determine
related: allocating-time-during-a-test, final-revision-checklist
practice: mathematics
---
Careless errors are among the easiest to fix. A short routine in the final minutes can recover marks.

## Check you have answered everything

Scan every page. Look for skipped questions, missing parts of multi-part questions, and instructions such as "answer both parts".

## Check names and numbers on the paper

Make sure your name or candidate number is on every sheet that needs it.

## Check calculations

- Re-read the question, then check that you answered what it asked.
- Redo key calculations a different way, such as working backwards or estimating.
- Check units and rounding instructions.
- Ask whether the answer is plausible. A person's age of 340 years or a probability of 1.4 signals a mistake.

## Check written answers

- Does each paragraph answer the question?
- Have you used the key terms correctly?
- Have you read for missing words, spelling of technical terms and unclear sentences?

## Check multiple-choice answers

Check that each marked bubble matches the answer you intended, especially if you moved between the question paper and an answer sheet.

## Do not panic-change

Change an answer only when you can state why the new answer is better.

## Quick recap

Answer everything, check identity details, recheck calculations, read written answers, and verify the answer sheet.

=== protect-sleep-before-exams
title: Why Protecting Sleep Beats Cramming
subject: study-skills
kind: Supporting explainer
description: Why a normal night of sleep before an exam usually helps more than extra late-night study.
vocab: justify
related: final-day-exam-plan, managing-exam-stress
practice: science
---
It can feel responsible to study late. For most people, staying up past a normal bedtime costs more in concentration and recall than it adds in extra reading.

## What sleep does for learning

Researchers have found that sleep supports memory consolidation, which is the process by which new information becomes more stable. Sleep also affects attention and mood, both of which matter during an exam. The details vary between individuals, so this article avoids exact numbers of hours; common guidance from health organisations is that most teenagers and adults need around seven to nine hours, but individual needs differ.

## What late-night cramming costs

- Slower thinking and more careless errors.
- Poorer recall of things you studied the same night.
- Increased stress and, for some people, difficulty falling asleep even when you finally try.

## A better plan

1. **Stop studying a while before bed.** Use that time to pack, relax and wind down.
2. **Keep your normal bedtime** where possible. A very different schedule can leave you more tired.
3. **Avoid heavy caffeine late in the day.**
4. **If you cannot sleep,** rest quietly. Even lying down calmly is better than getting up to study.

## If you are anxious

Write a short list of what you plan to do tomorrow. This can reduce the feeling of carrying everything in your head. If worry about exams is affecting your sleep for weeks, consider talking to a teacher, school counsellor or a health professional.

## Quick recap

Sleep supports learning and performance. A calm evening and a regular bedtime help more than more hours at the desk.

=== using-practice-questions-effectively
title: Using Practice Questions Effectively
subject: study-skills
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How to practise so that every question teaches you something.
vocab: identify
related: final-day-exam-plan, concise-revision-notes
practice: science
---
Practice questions are most useful when you treat them as diagnostic tools, not as scoreboards.

## Try before you look

Attempt the question without notes. Retrieving information from memory strengthens it more than rereading.

## Mark honestly

Compare your answer with the answer key. If you got it right, check that your reasoning, not only your final answer, matches the explanation.

## Classify your mistakes

For each error, decide which type it is:

- **Knowledge gap:** you did not know the idea.
- **Misunderstanding:** you applied an idea incorrectly.
- **Careless error:** you knew it but slipped, for example a sign error.
- **Misread question:** you answered a different question.

Each type needs a different fix. Knowledge gaps need review. Misunderstandings need a clearer explanation. Careless errors need a checking habit. Misread questions need a habit of underlining the command word.

## Keep an error log

A short list of mistakes and the fix for each is a powerful revision tool. Reread it the evening before the exam.

## Practise under realistic conditions sometimes

Occasionally do a set of questions with a timer and no notes. This shows you how you perform under pressure.

## Check where questions come from

Use questions from your teacher, textbook or exam board where possible. Questions on this site are original practice and are not official exam content.

## Quick recap

Attempt first, mark honestly, classify mistakes, keep an error log, and sometimes practise under timed conditions.

=== managing-exam-stress
title: Managing Ordinary Exam Stress
subject: study-skills
kind: Supporting explainer
description: Practical, low-risk habits for dealing with normal nerves before and during an exam.
vocab: identify
related: protect-sleep-before-exams, final-day-exam-plan
practice: science
---
Some nervousness before an exam is normal, and a small amount of arousal can help focus. This article covers everyday habits that many people find helpful. It is not medical advice. If anxiety is severe, lasts for a long time or affects daily life, please speak with a doctor, counsellor or another qualified professional.

## Before the exam

- **Prepare what you can control:** materials, route, schedule.
- **Move your body.** A walk or light exercise often helps people feel calmer.
- **Eat regular meals** and drink water.
- **Limit comparison.** Other people's study hours say little about how prepared you are.

## A simple breathing exercise

Breathe in slowly through your nose for about four seconds, then breathe out slowly for about six seconds. Repeat for a minute or two. Slow breathing helps many people settle, though it affects people differently.

## During the exam

- Read the whole paper first.
- Start with a question you can answer.
- If your mind goes blank, move on, then return. Often the answer comes back.
- Remind yourself that one hard question does not decide the exam.

## After the exam

Avoid long post-mortems of every question, especially before another exam. Plan something restful, then turn to the next task.

## Quick recap

Prepare what you can, keep basic routines, use slow breathing if it helps, move on from hard questions, and seek support if stress becomes overwhelming.

=== concise-revision-notes
title: Creating Concise Revision Notes
subject: study-skills
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How to turn long notes into a one-page summary you can actually use.
vocab: summarize, outline
related: using-practice-questions-effectively, last-day-revision-schedule
practice: english
---
Long notes are useful for learning. Short notes are useful for revising. Converting one into the other is itself a revision activity.

## Start from the exam's needs

Ask what you will need to recall: definitions, formulas, dates, steps in a process, comparisons. These go on your short notes. Background explanations can stay in your full notes.

## Compress without losing meaning

1. Read a section.
2. Close the notes.
3. Write the key points from memory.
4. Check against the original and add what you missed.

Writing from memory is slower than copying but is much better for learning.

## Use structure

- **Tables** for comparisons such as mitosis versus meiosis.
- **Numbered lists** for steps or sequences.
- **Formula boxes** with units and a one-line meaning.
- **Timelines** for history.

## Use your own words

Notes in your own words show that you understand. Include the exact wording only for definitions you must state precisely.

## Keep it to one page per topic or subject

If a page is crowded, you may be including too much. Choose what is most likely to matter.

## Quick recap

Decide what you must recall, write from memory, use structure, and keep notes short.
