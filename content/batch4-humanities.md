A clear essay structure helps the reader follow your reasoning. Most academic essays follow the same basic shape.

## The three parts

1. **Introduction:** introduces the topic, gives necessary context, and states your [thesis statement](/vocabulary/thesis-statement/).
2. **Body:** a series of paragraphs, each developing one point that supports the thesis.
3. **Conclusion:** restates the main argument in light of the evidence and explains why it matters.

## The introduction

A strong introduction usually does three things:

- Opens with context or a focused statement about the topic.
- Narrows to the specific question.
- Ends with a thesis that answers the question.

Avoid long background passages and avoid announcing "In this essay I will..." if a more direct statement is possible.

## Body paragraphs

A common pattern is **PEEL**:

- **Point:** a [topic sentence](/vocabulary/topic-sentence/) stating the paragraph's main idea.
- **Evidence:** a quotation, fact, example or data.
- **Explanation:** how the evidence supports the point.
- **Link:** how this connects back to the thesis or leads to the next point.

**Example for a paragraph about a novel.** The point: the setting reflects the character's isolation. The evidence: a short quotation describing an empty house. The explanation: how the description conveys loneliness. The link: how this supports the thesis about isolation.

## Transitions

Use words and phrases that show relationships: *however* (contrast), *therefore* (result), *for example* (illustration), *in addition* (continuation), *similarly* (comparison).

## The conclusion

Summarise the reasoning, not just the points. Say what follows from your argument. Do not introduce a major new point.

## Common mistakes

- A thesis that is a fact rather than an arguable claim.
- Paragraphs with several unrelated points.
- Evidence with no explanation.
- A conclusion that repeats the introduction word for word.

## Quick recap

Introduction with a thesis, one point per paragraph with evidence and explanation, linking words between ideas, and a conclusion that answers the question.

=== thesis-statements
title: Writing a Strong Thesis Statement
subject: english

vocab: thesis-statement, argument
related: essay-structure-basics
practice: english
---
A [thesis statement](/vocabulary/thesis-statement/) is a sentence or two that states the central claim of your essay. It is your answer to the question, which the rest of the essay supports.

## What makes a thesis strong

- **Arguable:** a reasonable person could disagree.
- **Specific:** it names exactly what you are claiming.
- **Focused:** it can be developed in the length of the essay.
- **Answers the question** if there is one.

## Weak and strong examples

| Weak | Why | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "Pollution is bad." | Too general; nobody disagrees. | "Local governments should limit private car use in city centres, because doing so reduces air pollution more cost-effectively than increasing public transport capacity alone." |
| "This essay is about *Macbeth*." | An announcement, not a claim. | "In *Macbeth*, ambition becomes destructive because it replaces moral restraint with fear." |
| "The Industrial Revolution had many effects." | Vague. | "The Industrial Revolution transformed Britain's economy mainly by shifting work from home to factories, which changed family and urban life." |

## A formula to start with

Topic + claim + because/by reasoning. For example: "Remote work improves productivity for many employees because it reduces commuting time and allows focused work."

A formula is a starting point. Revise it until it sounds natural.

## Revise as you write

Your first thesis is a working draft. If your evidence leads to a different conclusion, update the thesis so it matches the essay.

## Quick recap

State an arguable, specific, focused claim that answers the question. Revise it to match your evidence.

=== commonly-confused-words
title: Commonly Confused Words
subject: english
kind: Vocabulary reference
description: Pairs of words that are often mixed up, with the difference and an example for each.
vocab: affect-effect, their-there-theyre, its-its, then-than
related: metaphor-vs-simile

---
These pairs cause many errors in writing. Learn the difference for each.

## affect and effect

- **Affect** (usually a verb): to influence. *The weather affected the match.*
- **Effect** (usually a noun): a result. *The effect of the weather was a delay.*
- A quick test: if you can put "the" before the word, it is probably *effect*. (Both words have less common uses, such as "effect change", meaning to bring it about.)

## their, there, they're

- **Their:** belonging to them. *Their books are on the table.*
- **There:** a place or the existence of something. *The books are over there. There is a problem.*
- **They're:** they are. *They're late.*

## its and it's

- **Its:** belonging to it. *The dog wagged its tail.*
- **It's:** it is or it has. *It's raining.*

## then and than

- **Then:** at that time, or next. *We ate, then we left.*
- **Than:** used in comparisons. *She is taller than her brother.*

## your and you're

- **Your:** belonging to you.
- **You're:** you are.

## principal and principle

- **Principal:** a main thing, or the head of a school. *The principal reason...*
- **Principle:** a rule or belief. *It is a matter of principle.*

## accept and except

- **Accept:** to receive or agree. *She accepted the offer.*
- **Except:** excluding. *Everyone except Tom arrived.*

## fewer and less

- **Fewer** is used with countable nouns: *fewer apples*.
- **Less** is used with uncountable nouns: *less water*.

## Strategy for exams

Read your writing aloud in your head. Substitute the longer form ("it is", "they are") to test contractions. If it makes sense, use the contraction.

=== primary-vs-secondary-sources
title: Primary vs Secondary Sources
subject: history
kind: Supporting explainer
description: What primary and secondary sources are, how to tell them apart, and how historians evaluate them.
vocab: primary-source, secondary-source, bias
related: historical-causation, evaluating-sources
practice: history
---
Historians learn about the past from sources. Sources are usually grouped as primary or secondary.

## Definitions

A [primary source](/vocabulary/primary-source/) is a first-hand record from the time under study or from someone who experienced it. Examples include letters, diaries, photographs, government records, speeches, newspaper reports from the time, and objects.

A [secondary source](/vocabulary/secondary-source/) is created later by someone who analyses or interprets primary sources. Examples include textbooks, biographies and documentaries.

## The same item can be either

Whether a source is primary or secondary depends on the question. A 1990 history of the First World War is a secondary source for the war. But if you are studying how historians in 1990 interpreted the war, it becomes a primary source for that question.

## Evaluating a source

Ask these questions:

- **Origin:** Who created it, when and where?
- **Purpose:** Why was it created? For whom?
- **Content:** What does it say or show? What does it leave out?
- **Reliability:** Was the author in a position to know? Could there be [bias](/vocabulary/bias/)?
- **Usefulness:** What can it tell us for this particular question?

A biased source can still be useful. Propaganda is unreliable as a record of facts but is useful evidence of what a government wanted people to believe.

## Corroboration

Compare a source with others. If several independent sources agree, the claim is stronger.

## Common mistakes

- Dismissing a source as "useless" because it is biased.
- Assuming a primary source is always more reliable than a secondary one.
- Describing content without evaluating origin and purpose.

## Quick recap

Primary sources come from the time; secondary sources interpret it. Judge sources by origin, purpose, content and usefulness for the question asked.

=== latitude-and-longitude
title: Latitude and Longitude
subject: history
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How the coordinate grid works, with key lines and a worked example.
vocab: latitude, longitude, equator
related: primary-vs-secondary-sources
practice: history
---
Latitude and longitude form a grid that lets us describe any location on Earth with two numbers.

## Latitude

[Latitude](/vocabulary/latitude/) measures how far north or south a place is from the [equator](/vocabulary/equator/), in degrees. The equator is 0°. The North Pole is 90° N and the South Pole is 90° S. Lines of latitude are also called parallels because they run parallel to the equator.

Key lines of latitude:

- Equator: 0°
- Tropic of Cancer: about 23.4° N
- Tropic of Capricorn: about 23.4° S
- Arctic Circle: about 66.6° N
- Antarctic Circle: about 66.6° S

## Longitude

[Longitude](/vocabulary/longitude/) measures how far east or west a place is from the prime meridian, which passes through Greenwich, London, at 0°. Longitude ranges from 0° to 180° E and 0° to 180° W. The 180° line is roughly where the International Date Line lies, with deviations.

Lines of longitude are called meridians and run from pole to pole.

## Reading coordinates

Coordinates are written latitude first, then longitude. For example, 40° N, 74° W is a point in the northern hemisphere and western hemisphere, near New York City.

## Degrees, minutes and seconds

A degree is divided into 60 minutes, and a minute into 60 seconds. Many maps and devices also use decimal degrees: 40.5° is the same as 40° 30′.

## Time and longitude

Earth rotates through 360° in about 24 hours, so 15° of longitude corresponds to about one hour. Time zones are based on this but follow political boundaries too.

## Common mistakes

- Reversing latitude and longitude.
- Forgetting N/S and E/W labels.
- Thinking lines of longitude are parallel. They meet at the poles.

## Quick recap

Latitude is north–south from the equator; longitude is east–west from the prime meridian. Always give latitude first and include direction.

=== historical-causation
title: Historical Causation: Why Events Happen
subject: history
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How to analyse causes and consequences, including long-term and short-term factors.
vocab: causation, analyze, evidence
related: primary-vs-secondary-sources
practice: history
---
Historians ask why events happened. Rarely is there a single cause. Good explanations separate different kinds of causes and judge their importance.

## Types of causes

- **Long-term causes** build up over years or decades. For example, economic inequality or political tension.
- **Short-term causes** happen shortly before the event. For example, a particular decision or incident.
- **Triggers** are the immediate events that set the outcome off.

## Categories

Causes are often grouped as political, economic, social, religious, technological or cultural. Grouping helps you notice what you may have overlooked.

## Judging importance

Ask:

- Would the event have occurred without this factor?
- Did this factor make other factors stronger?
- Did it affect many people or institutions?
- How strong is the evidence?

An answer that says "factor A was most important because..., although factor B also mattered because..." shows judgment.

## Consequences

Consequences can be short-term or long-term, intended or unintended, and different groups may experience them differently.

## Structure for an exam paragraph

1. State the cause.
2. Give specific evidence.
3. Explain how the cause led to the event.
4. Compare its importance with another cause.

## Common mistakes

- Listing causes without explaining how they led to the result.
- Treating the trigger as the only cause.
- Assuming that because one event followed another, the first caused the second.

## Quick recap

Separate long-term, short-term and trigger causes, link each to the event with evidence, and judge their relative importance.

=== what-is-an-algorithm
title: What Is an Algorithm?
subject: computer-science
kind: Supporting explainer
description: A clear introduction to algorithms, with a short example and the idea of efficiency.
vocab: algorithm, pseudocode, flowchart
related: variables-and-data-types, loops-and-conditions
practice: computer-science
---
An [algorithm](/vocabulary/algorithm/) is a finite, ordered sequence of clear steps for solving a problem or completing a task. A recipe, a set of directions and a long-division method are everyday examples.

## Properties of a good algorithm

- **Clear:** every step is unambiguous.
- **Finite:** it ends after a limited number of steps.
- **Correct:** it produces the right result for valid inputs.
- **General:** it solves a whole class of problems, not just one case.

## Ways to describe an algorithm

- **Plain steps:** a numbered list in everyday language.
- **[Pseudocode](/vocabulary/pseudocode/):** structured text that resembles code but is not tied to a language.
- **[Flowchart](/vocabulary/flowchart/):** a diagram with boxes and arrows.

## Example: find the largest number in a list

1. Set *largest* to the first number.
2. For each remaining number:
   - If it is greater than *largest*, set *largest* to it.
3. After the last number, *largest* holds the maximum.

In Python:

```python
numbers = [4, 9, 2, 7]
largest = numbers[0]
for n in numbers[1:]:
    if n > largest:
        largest = n
print(largest)  # prints 9
```

## Searching: linear and binary

- **Linear search** checks items one by one. It works on any list.
- **Binary search** works only on a sorted list. It checks the middle item, then discards the half that cannot contain the target, repeating until found. For a sorted list of 1,000 items it needs at most about 10 comparisons, because 2¹⁰ = 1,024.

## Efficiency

Computer scientists compare algorithms by how their running time or memory use grows as the input grows. This is often described with big-O notation. For example, linear search is O(n) and binary search is O(log n).

## Common mistakes

- Writing steps that are vague, such as "do the calculation".
- Forgetting to explain what happens when the input is empty.
- Using binary search on unsorted data.

## Quick recap

An algorithm is a precise, finite series of steps. Describe it with steps, pseudocode or flowcharts, and judge it by correctness and efficiency.

=== variables-and-data-types
title: Variables and Data Types in Programming
subject: computer-science
kind: Supporting explainer
description: What variables and common data types are, with short Python examples.
vocab: variable-programming, data-type, string, integer
related: what-is-an-algorithm, loops-and-conditions
practice: computer-science
---
In programming, a variable is a named place that stores a value. The value can change as the program runs.

## Creating variables (Python)

```python
age = 15
price = 4.99
name = "Sam"
is_student = True
```

## Common data types

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Integer (`int`) | whole numbers | `7`, `-3` |
| Float (`float`) | numbers with decimals | `3.14` |
| String (`str`) | text | `"hello"` |
| Boolean (`bool`) | true or false | `True` |

## Operations depend on type

- `5 + 3` gives `8` (integer addition).
- `"5" + "3"` gives `"53"` (strings are joined).
- `"5" + 3` causes an error in Python because the types do not match. Convert first: `int("5") + 3` gives `8`.

## Integer and decimal division in Python

- `7 / 2` gives `3.5`.
- `7 // 2` gives `3` (whole-number division).
- `7 % 2` gives `1` (remainder).

## Naming

Use clear names, such as `total_score`, not `x`. In Python, names cannot start with a digit or contain spaces.

## Assignment is not equality

In `x = x + 1`, the `=` means "store the result on the right in the variable on the left", not "x equals x plus one". After it runs, x is one larger.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing types without converting.
- Misspelling a variable name, which creates a new one or an error.
- Treating `=` as a mathematical equals sign. To compare, use `==`.

## Quick recap

Variables store values. Each value has a type that determines what operations are allowed. Convert types when needed.

=== loops-and-conditions
title: Conditions and Loops
subject: computer-science
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How if-statements and loops control the flow of a program, with Python examples.
vocab: algorithm, boolean, iteration
related: what-is-an-algorithm, variables-and-data-types
practice: computer-science
---
Programs are not limited to running lines in order. Conditions and loops control which instructions run and how often.

## Conditions

A condition lets the program choose.

```python
score = 72
if score >= 70:
    print("Pass")
elif score >= 50:
    print("Borderline")
else:
    print("Fail")
```

This prints `Pass` because `72 >= 70` is true. The first true branch runs; the others are skipped.

Comparison operators: `==` (equal), `!=` (not equal), `<`, `>`, `<=`, `>=`.

Logical operators: `and`, `or`, `not`.

## Loops

A loop repeats instructions.

**For loop** (a known number of repetitions):

```python
for i in range(3):
    print(i)
```

This prints 0, 1, 2. `range(3)` produces 0, 1 and 2.

**While loop** (repeat while a condition is true):

```python
count = 3
while count > 0:
    print(count)
    count = count - 1
```

This prints 3, 2, 1.

## Avoiding infinite loops

If the condition never becomes false, the loop never ends. In the example above, forgetting `count = count - 1` would make the loop run forever.

## Worked example: sum of 1 to 5

```python
total = 0
for n in range(1, 6):
    total = total + n
print(total)  # 15
```

`range(1, 6)` gives 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. The sum is 15.

## Common mistakes

- Off-by-one errors: `range(5)` stops at 4.
- Forgetting the colon or indentation in Python.
- Using `=` instead of `==` in a condition.

## Quick recap

Conditions choose a path. Loops repeat steps. Make sure each loop has a way to finish.

=== supply-and-demand-basics
title: Supply and Demand Basics
subject: economics
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How price is determined in a competitive market, and what shifts the curves.
vocab: demand, supply, equilibrium, scarcity
related: opportunity-cost-explained, inflation-explained
practice: economics
---
In a competitive market, prices tend to be set by the interaction of buyers and sellers.

## Demand

[Demand](/vocabulary/demand/) is the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price. The **law of demand** states that, other things equal, as price rises, quantity demanded falls. The demand curve slopes downward.

## Supply

[Supply](/vocabulary/supply/) is the quantity that sellers are willing and able to sell at each price. The **law of supply** states that, other things equal, as price rises, quantity supplied rises. The supply curve slopes upward.

## Equilibrium

[Equilibrium](/vocabulary/equilibrium/) occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. The price at that point is the equilibrium price.

- If the price is **above** equilibrium, there is a surplus (supply exceeds demand), which pushes prices down.
- If the price is **below** equilibrium, there is a shortage, which pushes prices up.

## Movement along the curve vs shift of the curve

A change in the good's own price causes a **movement along** the curve. A change in another factor causes the whole curve to **shift**.

Factors that shift demand:

- Consumer income.
- Prices of substitutes and complements.
- Tastes and preferences.
- Expectations.
- Number of buyers.

Factors that shift supply:

- Costs of production.
- Technology.
- Number of sellers.
- Taxes and subsidies.
- Expectations.

## Example

If a new study makes a fruit popular, demand rises. The demand curve shifts right. With the supply curve unchanged, the equilibrium price and quantity both increase.

If a frost damages the fruit crop, supply falls. The supply curve shifts left. The equilibrium price rises and the quantity falls.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "demand falls" when only the price changed. A price change moves along the curve.
- Mixing up a shortage and a surplus.
- Drawing the shift in the wrong direction.

## Quick recap

Price moves toward the level where demand equals supply. Other factors shift the curves; price changes move you along them.

=== opportunity-cost-explained
title: Opportunity Cost Explained
subject: economics
kind: Supporting explainer
description: What opportunity cost means, why it matters and how to identify it in an example.
vocab: opportunity-cost, scarcity, trade-off
related: supply-and-demand-basics
practice: economics
---
Resources such as time, money and materials are limited, so every choice involves giving something up. [Opportunity cost](/vocabulary/opportunity-cost/) is the value of the **next best alternative** you give up when you make a choice.

## Key point

It is the best alternative forgone, not the total of all alternatives.

## Example 1: personal

Maria has a free evening. She could study (value to her: a better grade), work a shift (paying 60), or see friends. She chooses to study. If working would have been her next best option, her opportunity cost is the 60 she would have earned, not also the enjoyment of seeing friends.

## Example 2: business

A bakery has one oven. It can bake bread worth 400 per day or cakes worth 500 per day, but not both at the same time. If it bakes cakes, the opportunity cost is the 400 of bread.

## Example 3: government

A government spends money on a new road. The opportunity cost is the next best use of that money, such as a hospital or school.

## Opportunity cost and decision-making

Comparing opportunity costs helps decide the best use of limited resources. If the value of the chosen option is higher than the value of the best alternative, the choice makes sense.

## Common mistakes

- Counting all alternatives instead of the best one.
- Ignoring non-money costs such as time.
- Confusing opportunity cost with the money spent.

## Quick recap

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative forgone. It exists because resources are scarce.

=== inflation-explained
title: Inflation Explained
subject: economics
kind: Supporting explainer
description: What inflation is, how it is measured, and how to calculate it.
vocab: inflation, interest-rate, consumer-price-index
related: supply-and-demand-basics
practice: economics
---
[Inflation](/vocabulary/inflation/) is a sustained rise in the general level of prices, which reduces the purchasing power of money. A single price rising is not inflation; the general price level must be rising.

## Measuring inflation

Statistical agencies track the cost of a representative "basket" of goods and services. The [consumer price index](/vocabulary/consumer-price-index/) (CPI) compares the cost of that basket over time.

Inflation rate = (new price index − old price index) ÷ old price index × 100.

**Example.** The CPI rises from 200 to 210 over a year.

- Change: 10.
- 10 ÷ 200 = 0.05.
- Inflation rate = 5%.

## Purchasing power

If prices rise 5%, an item that cost 100 now costs 105. Money buys less than before.

## Possible causes

Economists often distinguish:

- **Demand-pull:** total demand in the economy rises faster than supply.
- **Cost-push:** production costs, such as wages or energy prices, rise.
- **Expectations:** if people expect rising prices, they may demand higher wages and set higher prices.

Real situations often involve a mix, and economists debate their relative importance.

## Effects

Inflation can reduce the real value of savings, change who gains or loses on loans, and make planning harder. Low and stable inflation is generally considered less harmful than high or unpredictable inflation.

## Real and nominal values

A nominal value is not adjusted for inflation. A real value is. If your wage rises 3% while prices rise 5%, your real wage has fallen about 2%.

## Common mistakes

- Saying inflation means prices are rising on every item.
- Confusing inflation with the price level. If inflation falls from 5% to 2%, prices are still rising, only more slowly.
- Forgetting to divide by the old value.

## Quick recap

Inflation is a sustained rise in general prices. It is measured by index changes, and real values adjust for it.

=== citing-and-paraphrasing
title: Citing and Paraphrasing Sources
subject: research-writing
kind: Supporting explainer
description: How to use other people's ideas honestly, with the difference between quoting, paraphrasing and summarising.
vocab: paraphrase, citation, plagiarism, summarize
related: evaluating-sources, essay-structure-basics
practice: english
---
Academic writing builds on other people's work. You must show clearly which ideas and words come from other sources. Failing to do this is [plagiarism](/vocabulary/plagiarism/).

## Three ways to use a source

- **Direct quotation:** exact words in quotation marks, with a citation. Use sparingly, when the exact wording matters.
- **[Paraphrase](/vocabulary/paraphrase/):** restate a specific idea in your own words and sentence structure, with a citation. Roughly the same length as the original.
- **[Summary](/vocabulary/summarize/):** a much shorter account of the main points, with a citation.

A paraphrase still needs a citation because the idea is not yours.

## How to paraphrase well

1. Read the passage until you understand it.
2. Put the source away.
3. Write the idea from memory in your own words.
4. Compare with the original to check accuracy and that you did not copy its sentence structure.
5. Add the citation.

Changing a few words while keeping the original structure is not a real paraphrase.

## Citation styles

Different subjects and institutions use different styles, such as APA, MLA, Chicago and Harvard. The choice is usually set by your teacher or institution. Check their guide and follow it consistently.

Most styles have two parts: a brief in-text citation (for example author and year, or a number) and a full reference list or bibliography at the end.

## What does not need a citation

Common knowledge that is widely known and easy to verify, such as "Water boils at 100°C at sea level", does not usually need a citation. If you are unsure, cite.

## Keeping track

Record the source details when you take notes: author, title, publisher or website, date and page number. This makes citing easier later.

## Common mistakes

- Using exact words without quotation marks.
- Paraphrasing too closely.
- Citing in the text but leaving the source out of the reference list.
- Citing a source you have not read.

## Quick recap

Quote exactly, paraphrase in your own words, summarise briefly, and cite every time you use someone else's ideas.

=== evaluating-sources
title: How to Evaluate Sources
subject: research-writing
kind: Supporting explainer
description: A practical set of questions for judging whether a source is reliable and appropriate.
vocab: bias, evidence, credibility
related: primary-vs-secondary-sources, citing-and-paraphrasing
practice: history
---
Not every source is equally reliable. Judging sources is a skill you can practise.

## Questions to ask

- **Authority:** Who wrote it? What is their expertise or connection to the topic?
- **Purpose:** Is it meant to inform, persuade, sell or entertain?
- **Evidence:** Does it provide evidence, data or references? Can you verify claims elsewhere?
- **Currency:** When was it published or updated? Does the topic change quickly?
- **Accuracy:** Does it agree with other reliable sources?
- **Balance:** Does it acknowledge other views and uncertainty?

## Types of sources

- **Peer-reviewed journals:** reviewed by other experts before publication. Strong for research claims.
- **Books from academic publishers:** often well researched.
- **Government and international organisation data:** often authoritative for statistics.
- **News reports:** useful for current events; check the original reporting.
- **Opinion pieces and blogs:** useful for viewpoints, not for settled facts.
- **Social media:** can be a primary source for what people said, but unreliable for facts without verification.

## Lateral reading

Instead of studying a website for clues, open new tabs and search for what others say about the source and its claims. This is often quicker and more informative.

## Check the original

If an article cites a study, find the study. Headlines often simplify or exaggerate findings.

## Common mistakes

- Trusting a source because it looks professional.
- Rejecting a source only because it is biased, rather than considering how it can still be useful.
- Using one source for an important claim.

## Quick recap

Check authority, purpose, evidence, currency and balance, cross-check with other sources, and go back to the original where you can.

=== correlation-vs-causation
title: Correlation vs Causation
subject: critical-thinking
kind: Supporting explainer
description: Why two things moving together does not prove one causes the other, with examples.
vocab: correlation, causation, confounding-variable
related: logical-fallacies-basics, scientific-method-variables
practice: science
---
Two things are **correlated** if they tend to change together. [Correlation](/vocabulary/correlation/) does not by itself show that one thing [causes](/vocabulary/causation/) the other.

## Possible explanations for a correlation

1. **A causes B.**
2. **B causes A** (reverse causation).
3. **A third factor causes both** (a confounding variable).
4. **Coincidence**, especially with small samples or many comparisons.

## Example: ice cream and sunburn

Ice cream sales and sunburn cases rise together in summer. Eating ice cream does not cause sunburn. Hot, sunny weather is a third factor that increases both.

## Example: reverse causation

People who carry umbrellas are more likely to be wet. The rain causes both the umbrella and the wetness.

## How researchers test for causation

- **Randomised controlled trials:** participants are randomly assigned to groups, so other factors are, on average, balanced.
- **Controlling for confounders** in observational studies using statistical methods.
- **Temporal order:** the cause must come before the effect.
- **Mechanism:** a plausible way the cause could produce the effect.
- **Replication:** repeated results in different studies.

## Questions to ask about any claim

- Is the claim about correlation or about cause?
- Could a third factor explain both?
- Could the direction be reversed?
- How big was the sample, and how were people chosen?

## Common mistakes

- Concluding that A caused B from a graph alone.
- Saying "correlation proves nothing". Correlation can be a useful clue and a reason to investigate.

## Quick recap

Correlation shows association. Causation needs further evidence from experimental design, controls and mechanism.

A [fallacy](/vocabulary/fallacy/) is an error in reasoning that makes an argument weaker or invalid. Recognising fallacies helps you write better arguments and evaluate the claims of others.

A conclusion can be true even when the argument for it is fallacious. A fallacy shows the argument is weak, not that the conclusion is false.

## Common fallacies

**Ad hominem (attacking the person).** Rejecting an argument by attacking the speaker instead of the claim.
*"You can't trust her view on tax; she's rich."*

**Straw man.** Misrepresenting someone's argument to make it easier to attack.
*A says: "We should spend more on public transport." B replies: "So you want to ban all cars."*

**False dilemma.** Presenting only two options when more exist.
*"Either we cut all spending or the country goes bankrupt."*

**Slippery slope.** Claiming one step will inevitably lead to extreme consequences without evidence.
*"If we allow a short extension, soon no deadline will mean anything."*

**Appeal to authority.** Treating a claim as true only because an authority said so, especially when the authority lacks relevant expertise. Citing real experts on their subject is not a fallacy.

**Appeal to popularity.** Arguing that something is true because many people believe it.

**Post hoc (false cause).** Assuming that because B followed A, A caused B.

**Circular reasoning.** The conclusion is assumed in the premise.
*"The book is true because it says it is true."*

**Hasty generalisation.** Drawing a broad conclusion from too few examples.

## How to respond

- Identify the claim and the reasons given.
- Ask whether the reasons actually support the claim.
- Name the weakness politely and explain why.

## Quick recap

A fallacy is a flaw in reasoning. Learn the common types, check whether reasons support conclusions, and remember a flawed argument does not make a conclusion false.
