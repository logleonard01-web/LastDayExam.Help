Metaphors and similes are both figures of speech that compare one thing with another. They differ in how the comparison is made.

## Simile

A [simile](/vocabulary/simile/) states a comparison directly, using *like* or *as*.

- *Her voice was as smooth as silk.*
- *He ran like the wind.*

## Metaphor

A [metaphor](/vocabulary/metaphor/) says one thing *is* another, without *like* or *as*.

- *Time is a thief.*
- *The classroom was a zoo.*

The writer does not mean the classroom literally contained animals. The metaphor suggests noise, disorder and energy.

## A quick test

Look for *like* or *as* making a comparison. If you find one, it is a simile. If the sentence states or implies that one thing is another, it is a metaphor.

Be careful: *like* and *as* are not always comparisons. In "I like pizza" or "As the sun set, we left", they do not create similes.

## Related devices

- **Personification:** giving human qualities to something that is not human. *The wind whispered.*
- **Extended metaphor:** a metaphor developed over several lines or paragraphs.
- **Hyperbole:** deliberate exaggeration. *I have told you a million times.*

## Writing about their effect

Naming the device earns few marks on its own. Explain what the comparison makes the reader imagine or feel.

**Example.** In "The classroom was a zoo", the metaphor suggests that the students were loud and out of control. It creates a vivid image and a slightly humorous tone.

A simple structure: *identify the device, quote the words briefly, explain the effect, link to the writer's purpose.*

## Common mistakes

- Calling every comparison a simile.
- Naming the device without explaining its effect.
- Treating the comparison as literally true.

## Quick recap

A simile compares with *like* or *as*. A metaphor says one thing is another. In analysis, always explain the effect.
