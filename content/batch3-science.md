## Key concept

[Photosynthesis](/vocabulary/photosynthesis/) is the process by which plants, algae and some bacteria use light energy to make [glucose](/vocabulary/glucose/) from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen. In plants it takes place mainly in the leaves, in organelles called [chloroplasts](/vocabulary/chloroplast/) that contain the green pigment [chlorophyll](/vocabulary/chlorophyll/).

## Equations

Word equation:

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (in the presence of light and chlorophyll)

Balanced symbol equation:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

## Where the materials come from

- **Carbon dioxide** enters through stomata, tiny pores in the leaf, mostly on the underside.
- **Water** is taken up by roots and carried to leaves in the xylem.
- **Light** is absorbed by chlorophyll.

## What happens to the glucose

Plants use glucose for respiration, convert it to starch for storage, make cellulose for cell walls, and use it with nitrate and other minerals to build proteins.

## Limiting factors

The rate of photosynthesis can be limited by:

- **Light intensity:** rate rises with light until another factor limits it.
- **Carbon dioxide concentration:** rate rises with concentration up to a point.
- **Temperature:** rate rises with temperature up to an optimum, then falls as enzymes become denatured.

A limiting factor is whichever factor is in shortest supply. Graphs typically show the rate levelling off when a different factor becomes limiting.

## Common mistakes

- Saying plants "breathe in carbon dioxide and breathe out oxygen" only. Plants also respire, using oxygen, all the time. Photosynthesis happens only in light.
- Writing that photosynthesis "makes energy". It converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose.
- Mixing up photosynthesis and respiration equations. Respiration is roughly the reverse.

## Quick recap

Light + CO₂ + water → glucose + oxygen, in chloroplasts. Rate depends on light, CO₂ and temperature.

Isaac Newton's three laws describe how [forces](/vocabulary/force/) affect the motion of objects. They apply well to everyday speeds and sizes.

## First law: inertia

An object stays at rest, or keeps moving at constant velocity in a straight line, unless a resultant (net) force acts on it.

[Inertia](/vocabulary/inertia/) is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion. A passenger lurches forward when a car brakes suddenly because the body tends to keep moving at the earlier velocity.

## Second law: F = ma

The resultant force on an object equals its [mass](/vocabulary/mass/) times its [acceleration](/vocabulary/acceleration/):

F = m × a

Force is measured in newtons (N), mass in kilograms (kg) and acceleration in metres per second squared (m/s²).

**Example 1.** A 1500 kg car accelerates at 2 m/s². The resultant force is 1500 × 2 = 3000 N.

**Example 2.** A resultant force of 20 N acts on a 4 kg trolley. Acceleration = F ÷ m = 20 ÷ 4 = 5 m/s².

Note that "resultant" means the overall force after combining all forces, taking direction into account.

## Third law: action and reaction

When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts an equal and opposite force on object A. These forces act on *different* objects, so they do not cancel each other.

**Example.** When you push on a wall, the wall pushes back on you with equal force. A rocket pushes exhaust gases backward, and the gases push the rocket forward.

## Weight and mass

Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kg. Weight is the gravitational force on it, measured in newtons: W = m × g. Near the Earth's surface g is approximately 9.8 N/kg (often rounded to 10 in simple problems). A 5 kg object weighs about 5 × 9.8 = 49 N.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking an object needs a constant force to keep moving. Without friction, it would keep moving at constant velocity.
- Believing the equal and opposite forces of the third law cancel. They act on different objects.
- Confusing mass and weight.
- Forgetting to use the *resultant* force in F = ma.

## Quick recap

First law: no resultant force, no change in motion. Second law: F = ma. Third law: forces come in equal and opposite pairs acting on different objects.

=== balancing-chemical-equations

A balanced chemical equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides, because atoms are not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. This is the law of conservation of mass.

## The method

1. Write the correct formulas for reactants and products. Never change a formula to balance it.
2. Count atoms of each element on each side.
3. Adjust the numbers in front of formulas (coefficients) to balance one element at a time.
4. Recount everything.
5. If useful, balance elements that appear in only one compound on each side first, and leave hydrogen and oxygen for last.

## Example 1: water

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

- Left: 2 H, 2 O. Right: 2 H, 1 O.
- Put 2 in front of H₂O: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Now right has 4 H, 2 O.
- Put 2 in front of H₂: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
- Check: 4 H and 2 O on each side. ✓

## Example 2: methane burning

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

- Carbon is balanced (1 each side).
- Hydrogen: 4 on left, so put 2 in front of H₂O: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
- Oxygen on right: 2 + 2 = 4, so put 2 in front of O₂.
- CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. ✓

## Example 3: magnesium and oxygen

Mg + O₂ → MgO

- Oxygen: 2 on left, 1 on right. Put 2 in front of MgO: Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.
- Magnesium: 2 on right, so put 2 in front of Mg.
- 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. ✓

## Example 4: aluminium and oxygen

Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃

- Oxygen: 2 on left, 3 on right. The lowest common multiple of 2 and 3 is 6.
- 3O₂ gives 6 O on left, and 2Al₂O₃ gives 6 O on right.
- Right has 4 Al, so put 4 in front of Al.
- 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃. ✓

## State symbols

Equations often include (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas and (aq) for aqueous solution.

## Common mistakes

- Changing subscripts, which changes the substance.
- Balancing one element and forgetting to recheck the others.
- Leaving a fractional coefficient when a whole number version is required. Multiply through to clear fractions.

## Quick recap

Count atoms, adjust coefficients only, recheck. Never alter chemical formulas.

A [cell](/vocabulary/cell/) is the basic unit of life. Cells contain structures called [organelles](/vocabulary/organelle/), each with a particular job.

## Animal cell

- **Nucleus:** contains genetic material (DNA) and controls cell activities.
- **Cytoplasm:** a jelly-like substance where many chemical reactions occur.
- **Cell membrane:** controls what enters and leaves the cell.
- **[Mitochondria](/vocabulary/mitochondrion/):** where aerobic respiration releases energy.
- **Ribosomes:** where proteins are made.

## Plant cell

Plant cells have all of the above and also:

- **Cell wall:** made of cellulose, gives strength and support.
- **Permanent vacuole:** contains cell sap and helps keep the cell firm.
- **[Chloroplasts](/vocabulary/chloroplast/):** contain chlorophyll and carry out photosynthesis.

## Bacterial cell

Bacteria are prokaryotes. Their DNA is not enclosed in a nucleus; it forms a loop in the cytoplasm and may include small rings called plasmids. They have a cell wall (not made of cellulose), a cell membrane and ribosomes.

## Eukaryotes and prokaryotes

Animal and plant cells are **eukaryotic**: they have a nucleus. Bacterial cells are **prokaryotic**: no nucleus.

## Comparison table

| Feature | Animal | Plant | Bacteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Yes | Yes | No |
| Cell wall | No | Yes (cellulose) | Yes (not cellulose) |
| Chloroplasts | No | Some cells | No |
| Mitochondria | Yes | Yes | No |

## Common mistakes

- Saying plant cells do not have mitochondria. They do, because plants also respire.
- Saying all plant cells have chloroplasts. Root cells, for example, do not.
- Describing the cell wall as controlling what enters and leaves. That is the job of the cell membrane.

## Quick recap

Know what each organelle does, and the key differences between animal, plant and bacterial cells.

## The pH scale

The pH scale measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is, commonly on a scale from 0 to 14.

- pH below 7: acidic.
- pH 7: neutral (pure water at about 25°C).
- pH above 7: alkaline.

Each step on the scale represents a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. A solution of pH 3 has ten times the hydrogen ion concentration of one with pH 4.

## Acids and bases

An [acid](/vocabulary/acid/) produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) in water. A [base](/vocabulary/base/) can neutralise acids; a base that dissolves in water is called an alkali and produces hydroxide ions (OH⁻).

Common examples: hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), sodium hydroxide (NaOH).

## Indicators

Indicators change colour with pH. Litmus is red in acid and blue in alkali. Universal indicator gives a range of colours that correspond to approximate pH values.

## Neutralisation

[Neutralisation](/vocabulary/neutralisation/) is the reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and water.

acid + base → salt + water

Example: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

Acid and a metal carbonate: acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.

Example: 2HCl + CaCO₃ → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

## Strong and weak acids

A **strong** acid ionises completely in water. A **weak** acid ionises only partly. Strength is not the same as concentration. Concentration is how much acid is dissolved per unit volume.

## Safety

Wear eye protection, handle acids and alkalis carefully, and follow your teacher's instructions. Concentrated solutions are hazardous.

## Common mistakes

- Treating strength and concentration as the same thing.
- Saying pH 5 is "slightly" more acidic than pH 6 without noting the factor of ten.
- Forgetting that neutralisation produces a salt as well as water.

## Quick recap

pH below 7 is acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 alkaline. Acid + base gives salt + water. Strength differs from concentration.

## Speed and velocity

**Speed** is how fast something moves, regardless of direction. **[Velocity](/vocabulary/velocity/)** is speed in a given direction, so velocity is a vector and speed is a scalar.

speed = distance ÷ time

Units: metres per second (m/s) or kilometres per hour (km/h).

**Example.** A cyclist travels 120 m in 20 s. Speed = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 m/s.

To convert m/s to km/h, multiply by 3.6. So 6 m/s = 21.6 km/h.

## Acceleration

[Acceleration](/vocabulary/acceleration/) is the rate of change of velocity:

a = (v − u) ÷ t

where u is the initial velocity, v the final velocity and t the time.

**Example.** A car speeds up from 5 m/s to 25 m/s in 8 s. a = (25 − 5) ÷ 8 = 2.5 m/s².

A negative acceleration means slowing down (deceleration), if the object is moving in the positive direction.

## Equations of motion at constant acceleration

- v = u + at
- s = ut + ½at²
- v² = u² + 2as

**Example.** A ball is dropped from rest. Taking g = 9.8 m/s², find its speed after 3 s.

- v = u + at = 0 + 9.8 × 3 = 29.4 m/s.

## Distance–time and velocity–time graphs

- On a **distance–time graph**, the gradient is speed. A horizontal line means stationary.
- On a **velocity–time graph**, the gradient is acceleration and the area under the graph is distance travelled.

## Common mistakes

- Using minutes with metres per second without converting.
- Confusing distance (total path length) with displacement (straight-line distance in a direction).
- Saying a negative acceleration always means "slowing down". It depends on the direction of motion.

## Quick recap

Speed = distance ÷ time; acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time. Use gradients and areas on graphs.

## The scientific method

Scientists generally work in a cycle: observe, ask a question, form a [hypothesis](/vocabulary/hypothesis/), test it with an experiment, analyse results and draw a conclusion. Conclusions can lead to new questions. The method is a flexible approach, not a rigid checklist.

## Types of variable

- **[Independent variable](/vocabulary/independent-variable/):** the one you deliberately change.
- **[Dependent variable](/vocabulary/dependent-variable/):** the one you measure.
- **Control variables:** the ones you keep the same so the test is fair.

**Example.** To investigate how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis in pondweed:

- Independent: light intensity (for example, distance of a lamp).
- Dependent: rate of photosynthesis, measured by counting bubbles per minute.
- Control: temperature, type and size of plant, carbon dioxide concentration.

## A fair test

A fair test changes only one variable at a time. Otherwise you cannot tell which change caused the result.

## Reliable, accurate and valid

- **Reliable:** repeating gives similar results.
- **Accurate:** results are close to the true value.
- **Valid:** the experiment measures what it claims to measure.

## Handling data

- Repeat measurements and calculate a mean.
- Ignore or investigate anomalous results, and explain why.
- Plot the independent variable on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis.

## Evaluating an experiment

Comment on:

- Sources of error and how to reduce them.
- Whether enough repeats were done.
- Whether control variables were really controlled.
- Whether the conclusion is supported by the data.

## Common mistakes

- Changing more than one variable.
- Confusing independent and dependent variables.
- Drawing conclusions the data does not support.

## Quick recap

Change one thing, measure one thing, control the rest, repeat, and conclude only what the evidence shows.
