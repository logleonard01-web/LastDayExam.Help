Correlation vs Causation

Supporting explainer · Critical Thinking · 1 min read

Why two things moving together does not prove one causes the other, with examples.

On this page
  • Possible explanations for a correlation
  • Example: ice cream and sunburn
  • Example: reverse causation
  • How researchers test for causation
  • Questions to ask about any claim
  • Common mistakes
  • Quick recap

Two things are correlated if they tend to change together. Correlation does not by itself show that one thing causes the other.

Possible explanations for a correlation

  1. A causes B.
  2. B causes A (reverse causation).
  3. A third factor causes both (a confounding variable).
  4. Coincidence, especially with small samples or many comparisons.

Example: ice cream and sunburn

Ice cream sales and sunburn cases rise together in summer. Eating ice cream does not cause sunburn. Hot, sunny weather is a third factor that increases both.

Example: reverse causation

People who carry umbrellas are more likely to be wet. The rain causes both the umbrella and the wetness.

How researchers test for causation

  • Randomised controlled trials: participants are randomly assigned to groups, so other factors are, on average, balanced.
  • Controlling for confounders in observational studies using statistical methods.
  • Temporal order: the cause must come before the effect.
  • Mechanism: a plausible way the cause could produce the effect.
  • Replication: repeated results in different studies.

Questions to ask about any claim

  • Is the claim about correlation or about cause?
  • Could a third factor explain both?
  • Could the direction be reversed?
  • How big was the sample, and how were people chosen?

Common mistakes

  • Concluding that A caused B from a graph alone.
  • Saying "correlation proves nothing". Correlation can be a useful clue and a reason to investigate.

Quick recap

Correlation shows association. Causation needs further evidence from experimental design, controls and mechanism.

Key vocabulary

  • CorrelationA relationship in which two variables tend to change together.
  • CausationA relationship where one event or factor brings about another.
  • Confounding variableA third factor that influences both of the variables being studied and creates a misleading link.
