The Pythagorean Theorem

Supporting explainer · Mathematics · 1 min read

What the theorem says, when it applies, and how to use it to find missing sides.

On this page
  • When to use it
  • Finding the hypotenuse
  • Finding a shorter side
  • Pythagorean triples
  • Checking whether a triangle is right-angled
  • Common mistakes
  • Quick recap

In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides:

a² + b² = c²

where c is the hypotenuse, the side opposite the right angle and the longest side.

When to use it

Only for right-angled triangles. If a triangle has no right angle, you need other tools such as the sine or cosine rule.

Finding the hypotenuse

Example. The shorter sides are 6 and 8.

  • c² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100.
  • c = √100 = 10.

Finding a shorter side

Example. The hypotenuse is 13 and one side is 5.

  • 5² + b² = 13², so 25 + b² = 169.
  • b² = 144.
  • b = 12.

Pythagorean triples

Sets of whole numbers that satisfy the theorem include 3, 4, 5; 5, 12, 13; and 8, 15, 17. Multiples of these, such as 6, 8, 10, also work.

Checking whether a triangle is right-angled

Sides 7, 24, 25: 7² + 24² = 49 + 576 = 625 = 25². Yes, it is a right-angled triangle.

Common mistakes

  • Adding the wrong sides. The hypotenuse is on one side of the equation alone.
  • Forgetting to take the square root at the end.
  • Using it for non-right triangles.

Quick recap

a² + b² = c² for right triangles. Identify the hypotenuse first, substitute, and take the square root.

Key vocabulary

  • HypotenuseThe longest side of a right-angled triangle, opposite the right angle.
  • Right angleAn angle of exactly 90 degrees.
