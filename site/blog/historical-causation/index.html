Historical Causation: Why Events Happen

Supporting explainer · History and Geography · 1 min read

How to analyse causes and consequences, including long-term and short-term factors.

On this page
  • Types of causes
  • Categories
  • Judging importance
  • Consequences
  • Structure for an exam paragraph
  • Common mistakes
  • Quick recap

Historians ask why events happened. Rarely is there a single cause. Good explanations separate different kinds of causes and judge their importance.

Types of causes

  • Long-term causes build up over years or decades. For example, economic inequality or political tension.
  • Short-term causes happen shortly before the event. For example, a particular decision or incident.
  • Triggers are the immediate events that set the outcome off.

Categories

Causes are often grouped as political, economic, social, religious, technological or cultural. Grouping helps you notice what you may have overlooked.

Judging importance

Ask:

  • Would the event have occurred without this factor?
  • Did this factor make other factors stronger?
  • Did it affect many people or institutions?
  • How strong is the evidence?

An answer that says "factor A was most important because..., although factor B also mattered because..." shows judgment.

Consequences

Consequences can be short-term or long-term, intended or unintended, and different groups may experience them differently.

Structure for an exam paragraph

  1. State the cause.
  2. Give specific evidence.
  3. Explain how the cause led to the event.
  4. Compare its importance with another cause.

Common mistakes

  • Listing causes without explaining how they led to the result.
  • Treating the trigger as the only cause.
  • Assuming that because one event followed another, the first caused the second.

Quick recap

Separate long-term, short-term and trigger causes, link each to the event with evidence, and judge their relative importance.

Key vocabulary

  • CausationA relationship where one event or factor brings about another.
  • AnalyzeBreak something into parts and examine how those parts relate to each other.
  • EvidenceFacts, data, examples or sources used to support a claim.
